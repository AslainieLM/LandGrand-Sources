## Readability and subsection openings

Apply these requirements whenever drafting or revising Chapters I–III. They implement D-016 and [[Sources/Chapter I Readability Feedback 2026-10-28|the user's readability feedback]].

1. **Use basic academic English.** Choose familiar, precise words, direct verbs, and one main idea per sentence. Split sentences that combine several ideas. Keep necessary technical terms and explain unfamiliar ones when first used. Read each paragraph for a clear topic and a useful connection to the next paragraph.
2. **Introduce every section and subsection.** Place a separate, short introductory paragraph immediately after each heading and before detailed explanations or bullets. Begin its first sentence with the exact words **“This section”**, including under nested subsections such as Scope and Limitations. Usually 2–3 sentences should explain the topic and what the discussion will cover. In Chapter I, check Project Context, Purpose and Description, Objectives, Scope and Limitations, Scope, and Limitations. Keep the Objectives introduction separate from the general objective.
3. **Summarize the survey to explain the problem.** State the survey date, sample size, and grade coverage once in Project Context. Select only the figures needed for the argument. Report a finding as a count or percentage once, with the relevant denominator clear. Explain its meaning rather than listing every answer category. Keep full distributions and calculations in the source notes.
4. **Preserve evidence limits.** Include mixed or opposing views when they affect the interpretation. Retain the cross-grade coverage, small Grade 5 groups, and distinction between adult views and measured learning. A shorter summary must remain faithful to the data.
5. **Review before delivery.** Read the complete text for clarity, check every heading's opening paragraph, and remove repeated tally figures or repeated explanations. Verify the final Word pages and the two-page Context requirement. Meet required length through relevant discussion, not padding.

Use normal academic prose in the manuscript; the concise chat preference applies to conversation. These rules change presentation, not project scope or the original research data.

Use literature to establish the foundation and inform the research problem, research design, sources, and later comparisons. Select recent, relevant, objective material grounded in original facts or data. The deck sets no fixed publication-year cutoff or exact literature count. Evidence: [[Sources/Chapter 1-3 Guidelines Extract#Slide 30|slides 30–31]]. Select an actual theory only after reading and citing its source; no theory has been selected for LandGrand in the supplied files.

### Related Studies

For related projects or systems, describe each project, maintain continuity, and consider an inventory, comparative matrix, and appropriately attributed screenshots. The deck says to **consider 3–6 related studies/projects**; this is guidance, not a hard minimum stated in the PDF. Evidence: [[Sources/Chapter 1-3 Guidelines Extract#Slide 31|slide 31]].

A useful comparison for this project would examine audience, subject coverage, learning activities, platform, offline behavior, strengths, and gaps. These comparison dimensions are drafting suggestions. Supply real related-study references before writing comparisons or novelty claims.
